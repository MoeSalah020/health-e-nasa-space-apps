01

Earth Baseline — 1 ATM & 1 G

On Earth, gravity keeps everything where it should be. Your blood pools a little in your legs, your heart pumps a steady beat, and your bones stay strong because they carry your weight all day long. This is your body's happy place.

At 1 g and 1 atm, the cardiovascular system maintains a hydrostatic gradient between head and feet. Baroreceptors keep mean arterial pressure near 93 mmHg, and bone remodelling stays balanced under normal mechanical loading (Wolff's law).

Source: NASA Human Research Program — Cardiovascular & Musculoskeletal Risk roadmaps.

02

Reaching Orbit — Launch G-Forces

Blast off! During launch you get squished into your seat — up to 4.5 times your normal body weight. Your heart races to push blood all the way up to your brain.

Soyuz and Falcon 9 ascent profiles peak around 4–4.5 g in the +Gx (chest-to-back) axis. Cardiac output roughly doubles, heart rate rises 20–40 bpm, and lung volume compresses, increasing the work of breathing.

Source: NASA HRP — Acceleration & G-Transition Physiology.

03

Reaching Zero-G — The Fluid Shift

In space, the blood and water in your body float upward — about 2 litres moves from your legs to your head! Your face gets puffy and your legs get skinny. Astronauts call it "puffy face, chicken legs".

Microgravity removes the hydrostatic gradient: ~2 L of fluid cephalad-shifts within hours. Jugular venous distension, facial oedema and a 10–30 % decrease in leg volume follow. Intracranial pressure rises, and Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS) is observed in a majority of long-duration crew.

Source: NASA HRP — Fluid Shifts experiment (ISS), SANS evidence base.

04

Long-Term Station Life — Bones & Muscles

Without gravity pulling on your bones, they get lazy. Astronauts lose about 1.5 % of bone density each month — that's like ten years of ageing packed into six months! But two hours of exercise a day on the ARED machine helps a lot.

Bone mineral density falls ~1–1.5 %/month in hip and spine during early ISS missions without countermeasures, while muscle cross-sectional area drops 1–5 %/week. The current ISS protocol — ~2 h/day of resistive (ARED) and aerobic exercise — limits BMD loss to under 1 %/month.

Source: NASA HRP — Bone & Muscle Loss risk; ISS ARED countermeasure data.

05

Unprotected Vacuum Exposure

Movies show astronauts exploding in space — that's not what happens! Real space is a vacuum, so the air rushes out of your lungs very fast, but your skin is tough and keeps you in one piece. You'd have about 15 seconds before you pass out.

Exposure below the Armstrong limit (~19 km / 6.3 kPa) causes ebullism — water boils at body temperature. In a hard vacuum, hypoxia causes loss of useful consciousness in ~9–12 s, and decompression of the body's ~1 L of lung gas can rupture alveoli. The body does not explode: skin tension and tissue strength contain the pressure differential. Survival is possible if repressurised within ~90 s.

Source: NASA — Physiological effects of vacuum exposure; Armstrong limit literature.

06

Earth Return — Readaptation

Coming home feels weird! Your body got used to floating, so standing up makes you dizzy. Your brain has to relearn how to balance — it usually takes two to six weeks.

Post-flight orthostatic intolerance affects a large share of returning crew: plasma volume is reduced 10–15 % and baroreflex sensitivity is blunted. Vestibular re-adaptation (sensory conflict, motion sickness) resolves over 2–6 weeks. Bone lost in flight is only partially recovered — some trabecular deficits persist for years.

Source: NASA HRP — Post-flight orthostatic intolerance & sensorimotor adaptation.

07

Bio-Dome — Microbes & Plants

Your tummy is full of tiny helpers called microbes — and they change in space! Astronauts also grow plants like lettuce in space farms, to eat fresh food and to make oxygen.

Long-duration flight shifts the gut microbiome toward a pro-inflammatory profile, with reduced diversity and increased intestinal permeability. Plant growth in microgravity alters lignin and cellulose deposition; NASA's Veggie and Advanced Plant Habitat have produced lettuce, radishes and dwarf tomatoes on ISS.

Source: NASA GeneLab microbiome datasets; NASA Veggie / APH crop results.

A Three-Act Journey

I

Terrestrial Harmony

On Earth, everything in the body has a place — held there by gravity, organised by it, and quietly kept running by it. Scroll to meet three organs at work.

At 1.00 g and 101.3 kPa, the body is a hydrostatically organised system: perfusion gradients, mechanical loading, and vestibular calibration all depend on gravity as a constant reference. Scroll to examine three systems in their baseline state.

Beat 01 · Cardiovascular

Blood that climbs against gravity.

The heart sits just left of centre in the chest. Every beat pushes blood upward to the brain and downward to the feet — but gravity is always pulling it back. Special one-way valves in the leg veins catch each downward surge and let it climb, one beat at a time.

Mean arterial pressure sits near 93 mmHg. A hydrostatic column of roughly 70 mmHg exists between head and foot in the standing posture; skeletal-muscle pumping and venous valves oppose gravity's cephalo-caudal gradient to maintain venous return.

62 BPM · 120/80 mmHg · 1.00 g
Beat 02 · Respiratory

Every breath is effortless.

Twelve pairs of ribs form a protective cage. Beneath them, the dome-shaped diaphragm pulls down and lets sea-level air pressure do the work of filling two spongy lungs. At 1 atmosphere, breathing is the easiest thing you do all day.

Ambient pressure at sea level is 101.3 kPa with 21% O₂. Diaphragmatic contraction creates roughly −5 to −8 cm H₂O of negative intrathoracic pressure; gravitational perfusion gradients favour the lung bases, naturally distributing ventilation-perfusion matching.

14 breaths/min · 101.3 kPa · 37.0°C
Beat 03 · Visceral & Vestibular

The body has a before and after.

Below the diaphragm, the liver sits on the right, the stomach curves on the left, and the intestines coil below. Meanwhile, tiny crystals in the inner ear rest against hair cells under their own weight — telling the brain which way is down.

Sub-diaphragmatic viscera are suspended in anteroposterior alignment by peritoneal ligaments. In parallel, otolith crystals rest on the maculae of the utricle and saccule at 9.81 m/s²; their tonic discharge is the neural signature of "down".

Otoliths calibrated · Adrenaline baseline

But the body was made for gravity. Every part of it expects to be pulled.

II

The Making of an Astronaut

Before anyone leaves Earth, they spend years teaching their body what space will do to it — deliberately, in machines built for exactly that. Three of these preparations tell the story best.

Pre-flight conditioning targets the three systems most stressed by ascent and microgravity: cardiovascular tolerance to sustained +Gx loads, extravehicular kinematics in simulated weightlessness, and vestibular adaptation to sensory conflict.

Preparation 01 · High-G Centrifuge

Learning to breathe at three times your weight.

A giant rotating arm spins the trainee at 3 to 4.5 times Earth's gravity. Blood gets so heavy it wants to pool in the legs, and the brain can lose oxygen within seconds. Astronauts learn a trick called AGSM — tensing every muscle and taking short, quick breaths to keep the blood climbing to the head.

Centrifuge runs replicate launch (+Gx, chest-to-back) and reentry (+Gz, head-to-foot) profiles. Trainees perform the Anti-G Straining Maneuver (L-1/M-1 AGSM): isometric lower-limb contraction with 3-second respiratory cycles to elevate mean arterial pressure at eye level and avert G-induced loss of consciousness.

Why it matters · Ascent peaks at 4.2 Gx
Preparation 02 · Neutral Buoyancy Lab

Learning to work when nothing pulls you down.

In a 6.2-million-gallon indoor pool, astronauts wear weighted spacesuits so they neither float nor sink. Over seven-hour dives they rehearse spacewalk repairs at the bottom of the water — the closest Earth gets to the weightlessness of orbit.

Neutral buoyancy balances hydrostatic forces against gravitational weight to approximate the free-float kinematics of Extravehicular Activity. Full-scale ISS mock-ups on the pool floor let crews rehearse task sequencing, tooling, and tethered body positioning under realistic crew time.

Why it matters · Every ISS spacewalk is rehearsed here
Preparation 03 · Parabolic Flight

Twenty-five seconds of freefall, forty times a flight.

A modified jet climbs steeply, then tips nose-down to trace a rollercoaster arc through the sky. At the top of each arc, everything inside floats for about 25 seconds. Astronauts ride forty of these arcs in a single flight to teach the inner ear what weightlessness will feel like — and to learn how to work while it lasts.

A parabolic profile alternates ~20 s at 1.8 g with ~25 s at 0.00 g, repeated 30–40 times per flight. Each parabola produces a controlled vestibular stimulus, letting crews habituate the otolith organs to unloaded states before orbital insertion introduces the same conflict in an unforgiving environment.

Why it matters · Space adaptation syndrome peaks in first 72 hours

Then, at T-minus zero, the training becomes real.

III

Body in Freefall

Eight minutes to orbit. Then a lifetime — or at least six months — in which gravity stops doing what your body expects. Scroll to watch the body adapt, in slow motion.

Ascent introduces peak +4.2 Gx compression; MECO removes it in an instant. What follows is a cascade of adaptations — fluid redistribution, axial unloading, and vestibular recalibration — that unfold across hours, weeks, and months.

Beat 01 · Ascent

Eight minutes pressed into the seat.

During ascent, the rocket pushes the body at up to 4.2 times normal gravity. The chest feels heavy, breathing turns shallow and quick, and every organ inside the abdomen is pressed toward the spine. The heart races at 130–140 beats per minute to keep pushing blood up to the brain.

Peak +Gx chest-to-back loading compresses the rib cage and diaphragm, reducing tidal volume and driving rapid, shallow breathing. Blood weight triples; cardiac output rises to ~9 L/min with heart rate near 135 bpm. Abdominal viscera compress posteriorly against the spine, and plasma catecholamines surge through the ascent window.

4.2 Gx · 135 BPM · Adrenaline surge
Beat 02 · Fluid Shift

Two litres of blood move up.

Main engine cutoff — and gravity is gone. Within hours, roughly two litres of fluid drift from the legs into the chest and face. Crews call it "puffy face, chicken legs". The brain senses fullness and starts to purge the extra volume, so the body ends up with less total fluid than it started with.

Microgravity removes the hydrostatic gradient. ~2 L shifts cephalad within the first hours, distending jugular veins and elevating intracranial pressure. The kidneys respond with a diuresis that reduces plasma volume by 10–15% over weeks. SANS — Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome — is observed in a majority of long-duration crew.

+2 L cephalad · −15% plasma volume
Beat 03 · Structural Change

Bone and muscle, unloaded.

Bones stay strong by being pushed and pulled every day. Without gravity, they stop being pushed — and start thinning at roughly 1 to 1.5% per month. Muscles waste the same way. Two hours of hard exercise a day on orbit only partly offsets the loss; some of it never comes back.

BMD falls ~1–1.5%/month in hip and spine without countermeasures, while muscle cross-sectional area drops 1–5%/week. Resistive loading (ARED) plus aerobic work limits BMD loss to under 1%/month. Trabecular deficits incurred over six months are only partially recovered after return; some persist for years.

−1.5% BMD/month · ARED · partial recovery
Beat 04 · Return

Coming home hurts a little.

Landing is stranger than launch. The body has unlearned gravity — so standing up makes it faint, and the brain has to re-balance itself all over again. It usually takes two to six weeks. The bone that was lost does not fully come back.

Post-flight orthostatic intolerance affects a majority of returning crew: plasma volume remains reduced and baroreflex sensitivity is blunted until reconditioning restores both over 2–6 weeks. Vestibular re-adaptation (sensory conflict, motion sickness) resolves on the same timescale. Bone lost in flight is only partially recovered.

Orthostatic intolerance · 2–6 weeks to readapt
End of the journey

The body is never really home. It's always adapting.

Every organ you met on this scroll was shaped by one constant force — and every astronaut who leaves Earth is a small, deliberate experiment in what happens when that force disappears. HEALTH-E exists to make that experiment legible to anyone curious enough to follow along.

The physiological adaptations explored across these three acts — hydrostatic reorganisation, axial unloading, vestibular recalibration, and structural remodelling — represent the current frontier of NASA Human Research Program investigation. Understanding them is not only essential for safe long-duration spaceflight; it is how we learn what the human body is capable of, and where its limits still lie.
